What's really going on

Why your gel chips - and why it's not your fault

You've switched three salons in six months. Every time it looks perfect in the chair - and by day seven there's a chip on your thumb. And you've started tucking your hands away in meetings without even thinking about it.

It's almost never you. Gel chips because the cuticle wasn't fully cleaned, the nail wasn't properly dehydrated, and the layer went on too thick. So the coating lifts from the edge - and it only gets worse from there: it catches on your clothes, breaks off in pieces, and within a week there's nothing left of that "fresh manicure."

Every chip isn't just a ruined manicure. It's another trip to the salon, another $85, and the creeping thought that it'll never be right anywhere.

Why the average salon keeps repeating it

In a rush, they push the cuticle back instead of removing it. A thin film of living skin stays at the base - and the gel goes right on top of it. It holds exactly until that skin starts to renew. That's where "one week" comes from: not because the gel is bad, but because it had nothing solid to bond to.

You change the salon - but not the technique. So the result stays the same.

How the Russian technique changes it

A Russian manicure is dry and done with an e-file. The cuticle isn't pushed back - only the dead tissue is carefully removed with a bit, leaving the living skin untouched. The nail stays clean and dry all the way to the edge. The gel goes on closer to the skin, in a thin layer, with no flooding - and now it actually has something to hold onto.

You see the difference not in the chair, but on day 7, 14 and 21 - exactly when the first chip used to show up.

What to expect after

The first days feel the same as always. The difference starts where the failure used to be: the edge doesn't lift, the shine doesn't dull, nothing catches. Most clients comfortably reach 4-5 weeks - and come back for a refill not because it fell apart, but because the nail grew out.
